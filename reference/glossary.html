    Week 1 Foundations

    Threat · vulnerability · risk

    A vulnerability is a weakness in a system; a threat is the person or thing that might exploit it; risk is the chance that happens times the damage it would do. You weigh risk to decide what to fix first — not every weakness is worth the same attention.

    Attack surface

    Every point where someone could try to get in — exposed services, open ports, logins, people. Shrinking the attack surface (turning off what you don't need) means fewer ways in to defend.

    Kill chain land and expand

    The ordered stages of an attack — reconnaissance, scanning, gaining access, maintaining access, covering tracks. The shorthand is "land and expand": get onto one machine, then spread from it. This arc is the spine the whole course follows.

    Defense in depth

    Stacking independent layers of protection — network, device, application, data, identity — so no single failure lets an attacker through. If one layer is bypassed, the next still stands.

    CIA triad

    The three properties security protects: Confidentiality (only the right people can read it), Integrity (it hasn't been tampered with), and Availability (it's there when needed). Most attacks break one of these three.

    NIST Cybersecurity Framework

    The defender's playbook, in five functions: Identify, Protect, Detect, Respond, Recover. It organizes what a security program has to do, end to end.

    MITRE ATT&CK

    A public catalog of real attacker techniques, each with an ID (like T1190). It gives defenders and pen-testers a shared vocabulary to describe, hunt for, and map out behavior.

    Where: attack.mitre.org

    Authorization & scope

    What makes hacking ethical: written permission that spells out exactly what you may test and how far you may go. Same techniques without it are a crime — scope is the line.

    Week 2 Reconnaissance & Footprinting

    Reconnaissance footprinting

    Building a map of the target before you touch it — its domains, addresses, people, and exposed services. Good recon is where a real engagement spends much of its time, because it tells you where to aim.

    Passive vs. active recon

    Passive means gathering information without touching the target — reading public records, so you leave no trace. Active means probing the target directly, which is noisier and can be detected. The line matters both legally and for stealth.

    OSINT

    Open-Source Intelligence — assembling a picture from public data: websites, social media, job posts, breach dumps. Cheap, legal, and often surprisingly revealing.

    WHOIS

    The registration record for a domain — who owns it, when it was created, contact details. A starting point for mapping who and what you're looking at.

    Tool: whois

    DNS

    The internet's address book, translating names like example.com into IP addresses. Recon reads DNS to discover a target's hosts and services.

    Tools: dig, nslookup

    Zone transfer

    A request asking a DNS server to hand over its entire zone (all records at once). It's meant for server-to-server syncing — but a misconfigured server will give it to anyone, exposing the whole internal map. One of the few active recon steps.

    Certificate transparency

    Public logs of every TLS certificate issued. Because certs name the hosts they cover, searching these logs reveals subdomains an organization never advertised.

    Where: crt.sh

    Shodan

    A search engine for internet-connected devices and services — it indexes what's exposed (servers, cameras, industrial gear) so you can find a target's front doors without scanning them yourself.

    Where: shodan.io

    Week 3 Scanning, Enumeration & Vulnerability Analysis

    Host discovery

    Finding which addresses in a range are actually alive before you spend time on them — the "who's home?" step.

    Tool: Nmap (ping sweep)

    Port scanning

    Probing a host to see which network ports are open — each open port is a door into a running service. The core move of active scanning.

    Tool: Nmap

    Service & version detection

    Going past "port 80 is open" to "Apache 2.4.49 is running here." Knowing the exact software and version is what lets you look up whether it has known holes.

    Tool: Nmap -sV

    OS fingerprinting

    Guessing the target's operating system from small quirks in how it replies to probes — Linux and Windows answer slightly differently. It narrows which attacks are even relevant.

    Enumeration

    Actively querying a service to pull out detail it will reveal — usernames, shares, software versions. Where scanning finds the doors, enumeration reads the nameplate on each one.

    Vulnerability scanner

    A tool that checks a system against a large database of known weaknesses and reports which ones it appears to have — so you learn what's likely exploitable without testing each flaw by hand. It's a finder, not an exploiter: it tells you where to look.

    Examples: OpenVAS / Greenbone (the Greenbone console drives the OpenVAS engine), Nessus

    Vulnerability analysis

    Taking what scanning and enumeration found and matching it to known flaws to pick a realistic way in. This is the bridge from mapping the target to actually breaking in.

    Week 4 Network Hacking & Sniffing

    Packet sniffing

    Capturing the traffic flowing across a network so you can read it. Passive sniffing just listens (easy on older shared networks); active sniffing is needed on modern switches, which only send each device its own traffic.

    Tools: Wireshark, tcpdump

    ARP spoofing ARP poisoning

    Lying to machines on a local network about which hardware (MAC) address belongs to which IP, so their traffic is sent to you instead of the real destination. The usual way to insert yourself into a conversation on a switched network.

    Man-in-the-middle MITM

    Positioning yourself between two parties so everything they send passes through you — you can read it, and sometimes change it, while both sides believe they're talking directly. ARP spoofing is one way to get there.

    Week 5 Cryptography

    The three jobs of cryptography

    Crypto exists to provide confidentiality (keep it secret), integrity (prove it wasn't changed), and authentication (prove who sent it). Almost every crypto tool is doing one of these three.

    Symmetric vs. asymmetric encryption

    Symmetric uses one shared secret key to lock and unlock — fast, but both sides must already share the key. Asymmetric uses a public/private key pair — anyone can encrypt to your public key, only your private key opens it — which solves the "how do we share a key" problem.

    Examples: AES (symmetric), RSA (asymmetric)

    Hashing

    A one-way fingerprint of data: the same input always gives the same fixed-size output, but you can't reverse it back. Used to check integrity and to store passwords without keeping the password itself.

    Example: SHA-256

    Salting

    Adding a unique random value before hashing a password, so two people with the same password get different hashes. It defeats precomputed rainbow-table lookups and forces an attacker to crack each one separately.

    Digital signature & certificates

    Using a private key to sign data so anyone with the matching public key can verify it really came from you and wasn't altered. A certificate binds a public key to an identity, vouched for by a trusted authority (CA) — the basis of HTTPS trust.

    Week 6 Wireless Hacking

    Access point & station

    The access point (AP) is the Wi-Fi base that devices connect through; a station is a client device (laptop, phone). Wireless attacks target the signal between them, which anyone in range can hear.

    WPA handshake

    The short authentication exchange when a device joins a protected Wi-Fi network. Capturing it gives an attacker the material to try cracking the password offline, away from the network.

    Deauthentication attack

    Forcing a connected device off the network with spoofed "disconnect" frames, so it automatically reconnects — and you capture the handshake as it does. The usual setup move before cracking.

    Tool: aireplay-ng

    WEP · WPA · WPA2 · WPA3

    The Wi-Fi security standards, in order of an ongoing arms race: WEP (broken long ago), WPA and WPA2 (crackable with weak passwords), WPA3 (current, much harder). Which one is in use largely decides how exposed a network is.

    Evil twin

    A rogue access point that impersonates a legitimate one — same name — to lure devices into connecting, so the attacker can capture traffic or harvest credentials.

    Bluetooth: BR/EDR vs. BLE

    Classic Bluetooth (BR/EDR) is built for continuous streams like audio; Bluetooth Low Energy (BLE) is built for small, infrequent bursts from low-power gadgets (wearables, sensors). They're different stacks with different weaknesses.

    Wireless cracking suite

    The collection of tools for capturing wireless traffic and attempting to recover keys — monitor-mode capture, deauth, and offline cracking in one toolkit.

    Example: Aircrack-ng suite

    Week 7 Network Defense Evasion & DoS

    Firewall NGFW

    The perimeter gate that allows or blocks traffic by rules (which ports, which addresses). A next-generation firewall (NGFW) looks deeper — into the application and content — not just the port number.

    IDS / IPS

    Intrusion Detection and Prevention Systems watch traffic for known-bad patterns. An IDS alerts; an IPS can actively block. They're how a network notices an attack in progress.

    WAF

    A Web Application Firewall sits in front of a website and filters app-layer attacks (like injection) that a normal network firewall wouldn't recognize.

    Network segmentation

    Dividing a network into separated zones so a breach in one can't freely reach the rest. It turns one compromise into a contained problem instead of a company-wide one.

    Honeypot

    A decoy system with no real purpose but to be touched — since no legitimate user should ever connect to it, any contact is a high-confidence alarm.

    Reverse shell

    Instead of the attacker connecting in (which the firewall blocks), the victim machine is made to dial out to the attacker — because outbound traffic is trusted far more. That outbound connection becomes the attacker's command channel.

    Lateral movement

    Moving from the first machine you compromised to others deeper inside the network. The perimeter firewall never sees it, because it's all happening inside.

    Exfiltration

    Getting stolen data out of the network without tripping alarms — the quiet final step, often trickled out slowly or disguised as ordinary traffic.

    DoS / DDoS

    A Denial-of-Service attack overwhelms a service so real users can't reach it. A Distributed DoS does it from many machines at once, making it far harder to block.

    Week 8 System Attacks, Malware & Persistence

    Privilege escalation

    Climbing from the low-privilege account you first land as (like a web-server service account) up to full control — root on Linux or SYSTEM on Windows. You get there by abusing one of three things: a code bug, a misconfiguration, or a reused credential.

    Foothold

    Your first access on a machine — usually a shell running as a weak, low-privilege account. It's the starting line, not the finish: everything else builds from it.

    Exploitation framework

    A toolkit that packages the code for breaking in and operating afterward, so you're not writing it from scratch. In Metasploit, an exploit module breaks in, an auxiliary module helps (scanning, checking a login), and a payload is what runs once you're in. You point it at a target, set the options, and launch.

    Example: Metasploit Framework · RHOSTS = the target you're attacking; LHOST/LPORT = your machine and the port the victim connects back to

    Payload · reverse shell · Meterpreter

    The payload is the code that runs after an exploit succeeds. A reverse shell payload makes the victim dial back to you for command-line control (that's why you set LHOST). Meterpreter is Metasploit's feature-rich version of that shell — file access, screenshots, and more from one session.

    Privilege-escalation enumeration

    Scripts you run after you have a foothold that automatically scan the machine for ways up — misconfigurations, weak permissions, passwords left in files, outdated software — and print a ranked list, so you find the path to root without hunting by hand.

    Examples: LinPEAS (Linux), WinPEAS (Windows) — "PEAS" = Privilege Escalation Awesome Scripts

    Practice target

    A machine built to be insecure on purpose — loaded with known vulnerabilities and weak settings — so you can attack it safely and legally while learning. (Don't confuse Metasploitable, the deliberately-vulnerable target, with Metasploit, the attacking framework you run against it.)

    Example: Metasploitable (a Linux VM; PostgreSQL on it is set up with a weak login to break)

    Persistence

    Leaving yourself a reliable way back in, at your elevated privilege, so a reboot, a dropped connection, or a patch doesn't lock you out. Common mechanisms: a new account, an SSH key, a startup/registry entry, a scheduled task or cron job.

    RAT Remote Access Trojan

    Malware that bundles the whole "operate the victim" job into one implant — remote control, a command channel, and (typically) persistence. Drop one and you have a dashboard over the machine: files, screen, shell.

    Example: DarkComet

    Command and control C2 & beaconing

    C2 is the channel an implant uses to reach the attacker for orders and to send data back. Beaconing is its tell — the implant checks in on a regular schedule, reaching outbound (trusted) rather than waiting for an inbound connection (blocked). That clockwork pattern is what defenders hunt for.

    Credential dumping

    Once you're privileged, lifting the passwords, hashes, and tokens the machine keeps on hand so users and services can log in — from memory, the local password store, or files. Far easier than cracking, and it fuels the move to the next host.

    Example: Mimikatz (scrapes logins from Windows LSASS memory)

    Pass-the-hash

    Reusing a stolen password hash to authenticate somewhere else without ever cracking it back to the plaintext — the system accepts the hash as proof. A fast way to spread using harvested credentials.

    Rootkit

    Malware that compromises the operating system itself so that the OS's own reports lie on the attacker's behalf — hiding files, processes, and connections. Since security tools trust what the OS tells them, there's nothing left to detect. The ultimate evasion.

    Packing

    Scrambling or compressing a malware file so that signature-based antivirus — which matches files against a known-bad list — sees an unfamiliar fingerprint. Same malware, new wrapper.

    Process hollowing

    Starting a legitimate, trusted process, emptying out its memory, and running malicious code inside its shell — so the task list shows a normal, trusted name while your code runs underneath it.

    EDR & endpoint protection

    Endpoint Detection and Response is modern host defense that watches how processes behave rather than just matching file signatures — so it can catch malware that packing has disguised. The reason attackers need behavioral evasions, not just a new wrapper.

    DNS tunneling

    Smuggling data out by hiding it inside DNS lookups — a protocol that firewalls almost always allow out and rarely inspect. Slow, but it slips past controls that would block a normal transfer.

    Domain controller

    The Windows server that holds every account's credentials for a whole network. Compromise it and you can log in anywhere as a legitimate admin — in attacker terms, game over.

    Malware taxonomy

    The familiar names just describe how it spreads and what it does: a virus infects files and spreads when they run; a worm self-spreads across the network with no help; a trojan is disguised as something legitimate; ransomware encrypts data for extortion; a RAT gives ongoing remote control. Real samples mix only the pieces they need. The next few entries define each one.

    Virus

    Malware that attaches itself to a file or program and spreads when a person opens or runs that host — it can't travel on its own, so it needs a human to launch it. The oldest category, and the reason "don't open unknown attachments" exists.

    Example: Melissa (1999) — spread through infected Word documents

    Worm

    Self-spreading malware that copies itself from machine to machine across the network with no user action — which is why worms spread explosively, sometimes worldwide in hours. The difference from a virus is that no one has to click anything.

    Examples: WannaCry (2017), Conficker

    Trojan

    Malware disguised as something the user wants to run — a game, a cracked app, an installer — so they launch it themselves. The disguise is the whole trick; once run, it does its real job underneath. Named for the wooden horse.

    Example: Emotet — began as a banking trojan hidden in document attachments

    Backdoor

    A hidden way back into a system that skips the normal login, left behind so the attacker can return at will without re-exploiting anything. Often what a trojan or RAT quietly installs once it's in — and a core reason persistence matters.

    Ransomware

    Malware that encrypts the victim's files and demands payment for the key to unlock them — a final-impact payload whose goal is extortion, not stealth or theft. The clearest example of malware built purely to do damage.

    Examples: CryptoLocker, WannaCry (worm + ransomware payload)

    Week 9 Web Attacks I

    OWASP Open Worldwide Application Security Project

    A nonprofit that publishes free, community-driven web-security guidance. Best known for the OWASP Top 10.

    OWASP Top 10

    The reference list of the ten most critical web-application security risks, updated periodically — the shared vocabulary for classifying and prioritizing findings.

    This week: A01 Broken Access Control · A03 Injection · A05 Security Misconfiguration

    Injection

    Any attack where untrusted input is treated as code or commands instead of plain data. The family includes SQL injection, OS command injection, and cross-site scripting. The fix is always the same shape: validate input, and encode or parameterize output.

    Cross-site scripting XSS

    A flaw where an app takes user input and later writes it into a page as executable script, so it runs in another user's browser with their session. The classic payload steals the session cookie. Fix: encode output, set cookies HttpOnly, add a Content-Security-Policy.

    Example: a photo caption set to a <script> tag · TweetDeck worm (2014)

    OS command injection

    Input the app pastes into a shell command and runs on the server. A ; or | lets the attacker append their own command — often opening a reverse shell back to them. Fix: call library APIs instead of a shell, pass arguments as an array, allowlist input.

    Example: a filename like photo.jpg; <command> · ImageMagick "ImageTragick" (2016)

    IDOR · broken access control Insecure Direct Object Reference

    The app confirms you're logged in but never that the object you asked for is yours — so changing an id in the URL (?id=123 → 124) hands over someone else's record. Fix: check ownership on every object, server-side. The #1 risk on the OWASP Top 10.

    Example: First American (2019) — ~885M files reached by changing a URL number

    Security misconfiguration

    A weakness that comes from an insecure setting, not buggy code: a public storage bucket, default credentials, directory listing left on, verbose errors that leak the schema, or an unpatched component. Fix: lock it down, change defaults, patch.

    Example: Deep Root Analytics (2017) — ~198M voter records in a public bucket

    Web shell

    A small script an attacker uploads to a web server that gives command execution and a persistent way back in — often the end goal of command injection or a file-upload flaw.

    Three-tier architecture

    The standard web-app shape: a web server (serves the page), an app server (the logic), and a data tier (database + file storage). Kept separate so a hit on the internet-facing tier doesn't immediately hand over the data.

    Week 10 Web Attacks II

    SQL injection SQLi

    Input the app glues into a database query, so your text runs as SQL. A quote closes the string and ' OR '1'='1 makes the filter always true — every row returns, private ones included; a UNION pulls other tables like usernames and password hashes. Fix: parameterized queries. OWASP A03.

    Example: Heartland Payment Systems (2008) — ~130M card numbers

    Parameterized query prepared statement

    The real fix for SQL injection: the query's structure is fixed in advance and the user's input is bound as a value, never as code — so a quote can't break out of the data. Kills every SQLi variant at once. Back it with a least-privilege database account.

    Server-side request forgery SSRF

    You give the app a URL and it fetches it from inside its own network — so an internal address (like the cloud metadata endpoint 169.254.169.254) hands back secrets you could never reach directly. The victim is the network's trust boundary. Fix: allowlist destinations, block internal ranges. OWASP A10.

    Example: Capital One (2019) — ~100M+ credit applications via the AWS metadata endpoint

    Broken authentication

    Weaknesses in how a login proves identity: no attempt limit, weak passwords allowed, no MFA — so automated guessing eventually works. Fix: lockout + rate-limiting, strong-password policy, and MFA. OWASP A07.

    Credential stuffing

    Replaying username/password pairs leaked from other breaches against a login, betting that people reuse passwords. Distinct from brute force, which guesses many passwords from scratch.

    Example: The North Face (2022) — ~195,000 accounts taken over

    Session cookie session token

    A random string the server issues after login and trusts on every later request as proof of who you are — because HTTP is stateless and forgets you between clicks. The token is your identity: steal it and you are that user, no password needed.

    Session hijacking

    Taking over a logged-in session by stealing its session cookie and replaying it in a request. The cookie is grabbed by sniffing an unencrypted connection or via XSS. Fix: HTTPS everywhere, HttpOnly/Secure/SameSite cookies, token rotation. OWASP A07.

    Example: Firesheep (2010) — grabbed cookies off open Wi-Fi

    Cross-site request forgery CSRF

    A trap page makes the victim's own browser send a request to a site they're logged into; the browser auto-attaches the session cookie, so the app acts on it. The attacker never sees the cookie — the mirror of hijacking (ride the session vs. steal it). Fix: anti-CSRF tokens, SameSite cookies. OWASP A01.

    Example: ING Direct (2008) — forced money transfers

    HttpOnly · Secure · SameSite cookie flags

    Settings that protect a session cookie. HttpOnly blocks JavaScript from reading it (stops XSS theft); Secure sends it only over HTTPS; SameSite stops the browser attaching it to cross-site requests (stops CSRF). Which flag stops an attack tells you which attack it is.

    Multi-factor authentication MFA

    Requiring a second proof of identity beyond the password — a phone prompt, a code, or a hardware key — so a stolen or guessed password alone isn't enough. The single most effective defense against credential stuffing and account takeover.

    Week 11 Social Engineering & the Identity Layer

    Social engineering

    Attacking the person instead of the machine — manipulating someone into handing over access, so you log in, not hack in. No exploit, no malware; the "vulnerability" is human judgment. Targets the innermost layer, identity.

    Phishing · spear phishing · whaling

    Lure messages that trick a victim into clicking a fake login or running a payload. Phishing is a dragnet blast; spear phishing is researched and aimed at one person; whaling targets a big fish (CEO/CFO) for a bigger payoff.

    Example: Google & Facebook (2013–2015) — ~$100M via fake-invoice BEC

    Vishing voice phishing

    Phishing over a phone call — often impersonating an employee to the IT help desk to get an MFA reset. (Smishing is the same idea over SMS.) The human help desk is the target.

    Example: MGM Resorts (2023) — a 10-minute vishing call, ~$100M

    Pretexting

    Inventing a believable scenario and identity — "I'm from IT," "I lost my phone" — to make the request feel legitimate. The story that carries every social-engineering attack.

    Authentication factors know · have · are

    The three proofs of identity: something you know (password, PIN), something you have (phone, hardware key), something you are (face, voice). Social engineering has a way to beat each — and AI now beats "what you are" too.

    MFA bombing MFA fatigue

    Spamming a user with push-approval prompts until, worn down, they tap approve just to make it stop. Defeats "something you have" with annoyance, not code. Fix: number-matching MFA, rate-limiting, FIDO2.

    Example: Uber (2022) — a contractor approved a bombed prompt

    SIM swapping

    Convincing a mobile carrier to move a victim's number to the attacker's SIM, so SMS codes and calls arrive at the attacker — defeating phone-based MFA.

    Deepfake · voice cloning

    Generative-AI fakes of a real person's face or voice — a voice clone needs ~3 seconds of audio (~$2), and video can be faked live on a call. Beats "what you are" at a distance. Defense: verify out-of-band, FIDO2, on-device biometrics.

    Example: Arup (2024) — $25M wired after an all-deepfake video call

    FIDO2 hardware security key

    A phishing-resistant "something you have": cryptographic challenge–response bound to the real site, with a private key that never leaves the device. A deepfake can fake a face but not the key on your desk.

    UEBA · SOAR

    UEBA (user & entity behavior analytics) learns each account's normal behavior and flags anomalies — a reset credential logging in from a new device and immediately hitting admin functions. SOAR then auto-responds: revoke, quarantine, alert. The defender's AI catches the behavior the disguise can't hide.

    Tailgating physical social engineering

    Walking through a secured door behind a badged employee — the in-person half of social engineering, alongside shoulder surfing, dumpster diving, and RFID-badge cloning. Fix: mantraps, visitor escort, clean-desk, a challenge culture.
