    Week 2 · Reconnaissance & Footprinting¶

    🧭 Kill chain · Phase 1 — Reconnaissance

    ATT&CK T1595 Active Scanning T1590 Victim Network Info T1589 Victim Identity Info

    What you'll learn

    • Recon = building a map — adding pins (systems and people) and filling each in
    • The six moves: WHOIS → DNS → crt.sh → zone transfer → Shodan → OSINT/people
    • Passive vs. active, and why you always start passive
    • How a footprint feeds every later phase — and how defenders shrink it

    🥤 Running example — coca-cola.com

    Every move below is shown on one real target, coca-cola.com, from public sources only. Click through the flow and watch a global company's footprint come together from its name alone.

    1. Recon is building a map¶

    Like the crew in Ocean's Eleven studying the casino before the heist, an attacker studies a target before touching it. The job is to build a map — and it has two moves: add pins (every system or person that could be a way in) and fill each pin in. The finished map is the footprint: the target's whole attack surface.

    • Systems — domains, servers, software, cloud & SaaS
    • People — employees, roles, routines
    • Passive first — read public sources; the target never knows
    • Then active — interact, and they can log you

    2. The goal: find a way in¶

    A company keeps its internals behind a perimeter — from outside, a black box. But some systems face the world on purpose: the web and mail servers in the DMZ. Those are the front doors. Recon is casing the building — which doors face the street, which are guarded, and who comes and goes — almost all from public information.

    3. The recon flow — click through it¶

    Six moves take you from a bare domain name to a full footprint. Click each step to see the tool, the command, and what it reveals about Coca-Cola. Notice they escalate — the first five are passive; step 4 is the one that crosses into active.

    STEP 2DNSthe addresses

    🪪 WHOIS — the "deed"

    Who registered the domain. A public record — you never touch the target to read it.

    Coca-Cola: owned by The Coca-Cola Company, via registrar MarkMonitor — a corporate registrar big brands use, with contacts locked down. (A cake shop's domain would leak a personal name and email right here.)

    whois coca-cola.com

    🚪 Door so far: none — but you know who you're up against.

    🧭 DNS — the address book

    DNS turns the name into servers, and reading it maps the front-facing infrastructure:

    • A → the web server IP (the front door)
    • MX → the mail gateway — Coca-Cola's is Proofpoint (…pphosted.com)
    • NS → the name servers — Coca-Cola's are Azure DNS
    • TXT → the SaaS stack — M365, Salesforce, Atlassian, DocuSign, KnowBe4

    nslookup -type=MX coca-cola.com  ·  dig +short TXT coca-cola.com

    🚪 Doors: the web + mail servers in the DMZ. (See how DNS resolves, and who hosts each piece, in §4 below.)

    🔎 crt.sh — the side doors

    You can't get a public TLS certificate without the hostname being logged — so certificate names are hostnames. crt.sh lists every certificate ever issued for a domain, surfacing the forgotten dev, staging, and sso hosts that are usually the softest way in. Each is a new pin.

    crt.sh/?q=%.coca-cola.com

    ⚠️ One net, not the only net: a wildcard *.coca-cola.com cert hides the specific names.

    ⚠️ Zone transfer — asking for everything (the one ACTIVE step)

    A name server should refuse to hand its full record list to a stranger — only its own backups may ask. A misconfigured one dumps the entire zone, including internal hosts.

    dig AXFR coca-cola.com @ns1-09.azure-dns.com → Transfer failed ✔ (correct, secure)
    dig AXFR zonetransfer.me @nsztm1.digi.ninja → dumps the whole zone (a practice domain)

    🚦 This is where recon crosses from passive to active — you asked their server to act, and it can log you. (Still not scanning — that's probing the machines directly, next week.)

    📡 Shodan — the front door up close

    A search engine for internet-connected devices. Look up the web-server IP and you learn the front door in detail.

    Coca-Cola: ports 80 (nginx) and 443 (Apache) on AWS us-east-2, with a list of CVEs for those versions — including a critical 9.8.

    ⚠️ Shodan implies vulnerabilities from the version banner — it hasn't tested anything; they may be patched. Leads to confirm by scanning, not proven holes. (See the Equifax case below.)

    👥 OSINT & people — the wider net

    Google dorking surfaces leaked documents; their metadata names vendors; LinkedIn maps the people.

    Coca-Cola: site:coca-cola.com filetype:pdf → a PDF whose metadata names Ascensus (their retirement provider — a partner pin). LinkedIn → the CIO and the IT org chart.

    🚪 People are pins too. (See the Target and bank-job cases below.)

    4. How DNS resolves — and who hosts each piece¶

    When your browser needs coca-cola.com, a resolver walks a chain (root → .com → the authoritative name server) and gets back the IP; then your browser connects to the web server. The twist: the name lookup and the web hosting are usually different providers.

    5. Passive vs. active — the framework¶

    Every pin answers two questions — how you got it and what it's about. Almost everything above is passive; the single active move was the zone transfer.

    Systems People
    Passive — never touch the target WHOIS · DNS · crt.sh · Shodan · dorking LinkedIn · job posts · a leaked PDF
    Active — they can log you zone transfer · ping · banner grab eavesdropping · a pretext call

    The rule: start top-left, squeeze passive dry, and go active only when you must — passive recon is essentially untraceable.

    6. It's real — three cases¶

    🔓 Equifax (2017) — one unpatched version

    Coca-Cola's server is probably patched — but don't assume, because one unpatched flaw is exactly how Equifax was breached. Equifax — a credit bureau (a credit-reporting agency like Experian and TransUnion) — failed to patch a vulnerability in Apache Struts, a web-application framework (a different Apache project from the web server on Shodan). The result: the personal data of about 147 million people — names, Social Security numbers, dates of birth — was stolen. Patch your internet-facing systems.

    🏭 Target (2013) — the weak partner

    Coca-Cola may be locked down, but a partner may not be — exactly how Target was breached. Attackers phished Fazio Mechanical, an HVAC vendor with weak controls, stole its credentials, and — because Target let the vendor in and hadn't segmented its network — pivoted from Fazio into Target, planted malware on the checkout terminals, and scraped about 40 million payment-card numbers. Your weakest link can be a company you don't control.

    🥷 The bank job — people are the softest target

    Red-teamer Jayson Street is hired by banks to break in. He pulls staff off LinkedIn, copies their badges from profile photos, and overhears a complaint at the staff pub about a VP furious over a botched update. The next morning he walks in with a box of donuts so someone holds the door, hands a teller a fake memo from that VP and a "patch" USB — and out of fear of the VP, they plug it in. Full remote access. Recon told him who to be and gave him a believable story. That's social engineering (Week 12).

    Defender's view¶

    How this looks from the SOC seat

    Passive recon is undetectable — it happens on infrastructure you don't control (search engines, WHOIS, certificate logs, LinkedIn). You'll never get an alert that someone looked you up.

    So shrink the footprint: disable zone transfers, strip document metadata, keep dev/staging off the internet, monitor your own certificate-transparency logs, and run OSINT against yourself. The less there is to find, the safer you are.

    Remember

    Reconnaissance = build a map from public sources. Add pins (systems and people), fill each in, and start passive. The footprint you build here drives your scanning, your exploits, and your social engineering — everything later is built on it.
