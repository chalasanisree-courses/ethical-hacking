    Week 11 · Social Engineering¶

    🧑 Identity · Hacking the Human · stop stealing credentials — just ask for them

    ATT&CK T1566 Phishing T1598 Vishing T1656 Impersonation T1078 Valid Accounts

    For eleven weeks we broke into machines. This week the target is the person who runs them — because you can patch a server, but you can't patch a human brain.

    The one idea for the week

    Every week so far has really been a hunt for one thing: a valid credential. We sniffed it, cracked it, stole the token. This week we stop stealing it and just ask a human to hand it over — log in, not hack in. A credential is an identity: hold it, and you don't hack in, you log in as them.

    The thread: it was always about credentials¶

    The identity layer & its three factors¶

    Social engineering attacks identity. Authentication rests on three factors — and each has a way in, now supercharged by AI.

    FactorHow attackers beat itWhat still holds
    What you know
    passwords, PINs, security Qs    		Phishing fake login pages · vishing ("read me your OTP") · answers dug off LinkedInDon't reuse passwords; phishing-resistant MFA
    What you have
    phone, hardware key    		SIM swap · help-desk MFA reset · MFA bombing (fatigue)FIDO2 hardware keys resist all of these
    What you are
    face, touch, voice    		Voice clone (~$2, 3 sec of audio) · real-time deepfake videoOn-device biometrics; out-of-band verification

    The attacks¶

    Click an attack to see how it works, how to defend it, where it's bitten in the real world, and how a pentester tests for it.

    🎣 Phishing, spear phishing & whalingdefeats "what you know"

    A lure message gets you to click a link to a fake login page; you type your password and it's captured. Three scales of aim: phishing is a dragnet — a generic blast to thousands, hoping someone bites. Spear phishing is researched and aimed at you — your name, title, and boss, pulled from LinkedIn. Whaling targets a big fish (CEO/CFO), where one catch authorizes a wire or hands over high-level access.

    Defense

    Don't trust links in email — verify the sender and open the site directly. Technically: email authentication (SPF / DKIM / DMARC), link sandboxing, and phishing-resistant MFA (FIDO2) so a stolen password isn't enough. And phishing-simulation training so clicks become teachable moments.

    Real world

    Google & Facebook (2013–2015) — one operator ran a vendor-impersonation (BEC) scheme with fake invoices and emails and collected about $100 million before it was caught.

    Spot it

    Check the real sender domain and hover every link before clicking; watch for urgency paired with a login prompt. In an authorized engagement you'd craft a spear-phish with the Social Engineer Toolkit (SET) and measure click-through — that's your lab this week.

    📞 Vishing & the help deskthe MGM pattern

    Instead of the employee, target the people paid to help them. Call the IT help desk, impersonate an employee — "I lost my phone, I need my MFA reset" — and the helpful agent resets it, handing you the account. That's pretexting delivered over voice (vishing). No malware, no exploit — you just logged in.

    Defense

    Blind help-desk PINs — the agent checks a secret the employee set without ever seeing it (pass/fail), so they can't be tricked into revealing it. Plus callback to a known number, quorum / manager approval for privileged resets, and a cool-off sandbox after any reset.

    Real world

    MGM Resorts (2023) — Scattered Spider vished the help desk, got an MFA reset, escalated to Okta super-admin → Azure admin, and deployed ransomware. A 10-minute call, ~$100M, and zero malware to get in.

    Spot it

    In an authorized test, see whether the help desk will reset MFA or a password without strong verification. Check for a callback policy, blind-PIN enforcement, and whether a single agent can reset a privileged account alone.

    📱 MFA bombingdefeats "what you have"

    You already have the victim's password, but MFA blocks you. So you trigger push approvals again and again — ideally at 3am — until, worn down, they tap approve just to make it stop. Nothing is cracked; the attack runs on fatigue alone.

    Defense

    Number-matching MFA — the user types a code shown on the login screen, so a blind tap won't do. Plus push rate-limiting, alerts on repeated prompts, phishing-resistant FIDO2 keys, and training to deny & report any prompt you didn't start.

    Real world

    Uber (2022) — a contractor was MFA-bombed into approving a login; the attacker then found hard-coded admin credentials and spread across internal systems.

    Spot it

    Check whether MFA is simple tap-to-approve versus number-matching, and whether a flood of repeated prompts is rate-limited or alerted. Tap-to-approve with no limit is the gap.

    🎭 Deepfakes & voice cloningdefeats "what you are"

    Generative AI clones a voice from about 3 seconds of audio (~$2) and renders a real-time deepfake face on a video call. The factor we trusted most — "what you are" — is now fakeable at a distance. Two models: a human using AI as a mask today; fully autonomous AI agents running the con next.

    Defense

    Verify out-of-band with something the AI can't know — a personal question (the Ferrari "book" question) or a pre-agreed code word for high-value requests. Require on-device biometrics / FIDO2 (not a face on a call) and quorum approval for payments and resets.

    Real world

    Arup (2024) — a finance worker wired $25M after a video call on which the CFO and colleagues were all deepfakes. Ferrari (2024) — a cloned-CEO voice call was caught only when the exec asked a personal question the AI couldn't answer.

    Spot it

    For any high-value request, does policy require a second channel or code word? Can one person authorize a wire off a video call alone? If yes, a deepfake walks right in.

    🚪 Physical / tailgatingthe low-tech other half

    The same levers — authority, trust, urgency — work in person. Tailgate through a badged door behind an employee, clone an RFID badge, shoulder-surf a password, or dumpster-dive for discarded documents. No network required — you simply walk in.

    Defense

    Mantraps / turnstiles (one badge, one person), visitor escort and badge policies, clean-desk and shredding, and a challenge culture where staff feel safe asking "can I help you?"

    Real world

    A staple of authorized physical red-team engagements — skilled social engineers routinely tailgate into "secure" buildings; it's the low-tech half of the CEH social-engineering module.

    Spot it

    In a physical assessment, test whether doors can be tailgated, whether visitors are escorted, whether badges are challenged, and what ends up in the trash unshredded.

    Why humans are the weakest link¶

    Social engineering isn't technical — it's psychological. Attackers pull on emotion so you act before you think:

    😨 Fear
    "Your account will be deleted — verify now."
    ⏰ Urgency
    "This offer expires in one hour."
    👔 Authority
    "This is the CEO — I need it now."
    💰 Greed
    "You've won $100,000 — click to claim."
    🤝 Trust
    "Hi, I'm from IT support, here to help."
    😮‍💨 Fatigue
    MFA prompts buzzing until you tap yes.

    Defender's view¶

    How you defend the identity layer

    Human judgment isn't reliable, so you defend with things AI can't fake, backed by policy and behavioral analytics:

    • Physical / local factors — FIDO2 hardware keys, on-device biometrics, blind help-desk PINs.
    • Procedural controls — quorum approval for privileged resets, cool-off sandboxes after any MFA reset, callback verification.
    • Behavioral AI (UEBA) — the attacker's AI creates a convincing disguise; the defender's AI detects the behavior the disguise can't hide — a reset credential logging in from a new device and geo and immediately hitting admin functions. SOAR then auto-revokes and quarantines.

    Best control: defense in depth — physical factors + procedural policy + behavioral analytics. No single silver bullet.

    Final Project — the MGM breach¶

    Your capstone applies the Week 12 reporting methodology to a real identity-layer attack: the September 2023 MGM Resorts breach (Scattered Spider). Attackers looked up a privileged employee on LinkedIn, vished the IT help desk, claimed a lost phone, got MFA reset, escalated Okta → Azure admin, and deployed ransomware — a 10-minute phone call, zero malware to get in, ~$100M in damage.

    Deliverable — a professional incident / pen-test report covering:

    1. Attack reconstruction — the full timeline, each step mapped to MITRE ATT&CK.
    2. Identity-layer analysis — why "log in, not hack in" made this so effective (no exploit to detect).
    3. Layered defense design — which controls (FIDO2, blind PINs, quorum approval, cool-off sandbox, UEBA) would have broken the chain, and where.
    4. The AI dimension — how deepfakes / voice cloning would supercharge the same attack, and what changes for the defender.
